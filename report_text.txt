ABSTRACT
In India, chronic lifestyle disorders—including type-2 diabetes, hypertension, and respiratory illnesses—are rising rapidly, heavily aggravated by severe urban air pollution. Although digital interventions can provide scalable preventive education, generic AI chatbots generate clinically ungrounded medical advice lacking adherence to verified national standards. Furthermore, non-English-speaking citizens face severe accessibility barriers when seeking health guidance online.
To address these challenges, we built Aarogya Sathi, an AI mobile health companion tailored for Indian users with multilingual support in Hindi, Marathi, and English. The system couples a Flutter client with a cloud-hosted FastAPI backend. To eliminate AI hallucinations and ensure patient safety, the assistant deterministically grounds all clinical guidance in 34 ICMR guideline monographs using an agentic RAG architecture.
Aarogya Sathi dynamically integrates real-time environmental context (live CPCB AQI and weather) with personal wearable telemetry (steps, heart rate, and sleep duration via Android Health Connect). This enables personalized preventive advisories, such as alerting asthma patients to avoid outdoor exertion during high-AQI periods. For critical safety, sub-100ms semantic guardrails prevent unauthorized medication prescriptions and immediately route acute emergencies (chest pain, stroke symptoms) to India's 112 helpline. The system is fully operational on Android, demonstrating 4.86/5.0 factual faithfulness and zero safety violations across 32 gold-standard evaluation cases.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background
The changing shape of India's health care setting. Gentlemen, the only epidemic of today is that of non-communicable diseases (NCDs)—diabetes and its complications followed by cardiovascular diseases due to sedentary lifestyle further aggravated by no physical activity and exposure to pollution: chronic respiratory illnesses. NCDs comprise nearly 63% of the total deaths in India [1] according to the recent Global Burden Of Disease (GBD) Study. One of the leading causes for this upward trend is from exposure to environmental risk. Out of them, air pollution is the 4th leading risk factor for death globally accounting to ~4.2 million premature deaths per year [9]. However, for urban Indian cities already prone to hazardous AQI ranges and exposure, air pollution is an unavoidable risk factor in the ongoing management of respiratory and cardiovascular health.
When it comes to digital literacy and accessibility, India is witnessing a massive smartphone boom. But there is one roadblock: Nearly 90% of the Indian population do not have English as a primary language [26]. When people search online for health information, they encounter nothing but language issues, or worse yet the mainstream internet delivers up generic western-centric advice that has nothing at all to do with their local context and dietary habits. As of now, no one-stop application exists for greater AQI tracking in real time, vernacular languages support and health advisory that are AI-driven yet certain as per the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines.
1.2 Problem Statement
There is a growing prevalence of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) along with dangerous environmental conditions among the urban populations in India. The available methods of raising digital health awareness among people confront the following basic problems:
1. Lack of Correlation Between the Environment and Vital Signs: Individuals who have chronic diseases like asthma, COPD, and diabetes lack any sort of proactive health counseling which correlates their vital signs to the environmental dangers like extreme AQI levels in the locality.
2. Medical Inaccuracy and Hallucinations: Artificial intelligence and search engines offer generic and unconfirmed medical advice which can result in facts' hallucination without taking into consideration Indian eating habits and other risk factors or national-level clinical protocols.
3. Language Barrier: Medical information and health services are mainly limited to the English language which excludes a lot of population segment which speaks other regional Indic languages like Hindi and Marathi.
Thus, the aim of this project is to solve the problem of designing an intelligent and vernacular-friendly digital health companion which gives medical advice based on verified ICMR clinical guidelines and uses live environment data and wearable health data and also ensures sub-second safety guardrails.
1.3 Objectives
To address these gaps, our team set the following specific objectives for the Aarogya Sathi project:
1. Build a localized clinical knowledge bundle using 34 structured ICMR guidelines.
2. Develop an agentic RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) pipeline that grounds all AI responses in the approved medical documents to prevent hallucinations.
3. Create a mobile application using Flutter that integrates with Health Connect to pull user wearable data (like Fitbit steps and heart rate).
4. Integrate real-time AQI and weather data APIs to offer environment-aware health alerts.
5. Support multi-language interactions specifically targeting Hindi, Marathi, and English.
1.4 Scope and Limitations
The scope of Aarogyasathi includes preventive care, lifestyle advice, and symptom checker for common diseases according to ICMR. An Android client and backend on the cloud were created. The application continually monitors the user’s health parameters using Health Connect API and gives him customized information depending on the air quality of the city he lives in.
However, there are certain limitations for this application. First, it can be used only for preventive and informative purposes. It is not meant to replace the work of a certified medical doctor or any emergency service. Second, the RAG pipeline operates according to 34 uploaded ICMR documents; hence, all the queries that lie outside of this domain can result in a fallback response. The application was developed only for Android devices; thus, iOS is not considered.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE SURVEY
In this chapter, we will consider previous work and systems in the area of digital health, impact of air quality, and AI architecture in healthcare. We analyzed different papers in order to assess the current state of technologies and the gaps that need to be covered by our project.
2.1 NCD Burden in India
It is well known that there is a transition from infectious diseases to NCDs. According to the Lancet 2020 study, "Vos et al. [1]", NCDs have become the major cause of death across the globe. As per cardiovascular diseases, "Roth et al. [2]" mentioned that there were 523 million CVD cases and 18.6 million deaths in 2019 across the globe. It is clear that India is at the forefront of this problem. In the GBD 2021 Diabetes study [3], it has been projected that India would have the highest number of patients suffering from diabetes in the upcoming decades. In addition, the GBD Obesity study [4] stated that there are 107.7 million patients with obesity in India and consequently there were 4 million deaths due to high BMI.
2.2 Air Pollution and Health
Now, moving on to the next section, environmental risks are critical in influencing public health in India. According to GBD Risk Factors research [8], air pollution is listed among the five most significant risks for mortality globally. Lelieveld et al. [9] estimated that exposure to PM2.5 contributes to 4.2 million deaths annually. For India, the problem is even worse. As reported by Cohen et al. [11], South Asia has the maximum PM2.5 pollution in the world. The consequences of such pollution for people’s health are dire. Salvi & Barnes [13] found that the non-smoking population of India has a 30% reduction in lung function when compared to their Western counterparts only because of air pollution.
2.3 Digital Health Interventions and ROI
Technology can help in offering services for preventive care not only from the aspect of medicine but also economically. According to Masters et al. [18], a 14.3:1 rate of return on investment is gained from interventions of preventive health care. Early detection of diseases and influencing lifestyle with the use of mobile applications will significantly reduce hospital costs.
2.4 RAG and LLM Safety in Healthcare
In health-related data analysis, AI safety plays a crucial role. Acosta et al. [19] addressed the significant safety hazards of multimodal biomedical AI systems, especially related to hallucination and incorrect diagnosis. The safety issues of early conversational agents were emphasized by Laranjo et al. [20]. To address this issue, researchers have started to use the framework of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). Siriwardhana et al. [21] presented how RAG helps in performing end-to-end domain adaptation without the need for fine-tuning the base LLM. Recently, Anuyah et al. [22] suggested applying KG-RAG in health care applications for better precision. Xia et al. [23] achieved an increase in accuracy of 43.8% with MMed-RAG for medical query answering. Methods for mitigating RAG hallucination were also provided by Priola [24]. We took inspiration from these studies while building our backend architecture in order to make our chatbot speak only according to ICMR guidelines.
2.5 Multilingual AI for Indian Healthcare
Translation systems have to be used since English is not the primary language of most Indians. IndicTrans2 [28], which is an innovation by AI4Bharat, has become a big game-changer for Indic languages. A study conducted in 2026 on conversational adaptation of IndicTrans2 [29] resulted in +6.2 chrF improvements for conversation translation. What’s more, the CLINIC benchmark [31], which assessed multilingual LLM reliability, proved that the ability to understand one’s native language in clinical settings has improved.
2.6 Agentic AI Architectures
While the old approach involved only prompt-response systems, agentic artificial intelligence is being used now. The results of tests conducted on the AgentDS Healthcare Benchmark [36] show that dividing complicated tasks into smaller components such as retriever, calculator, or API caller leads to better performance compared to agentic AI guided by humans. In our project, we employed a ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) pipeline.
2.7 Data Privacy and Regulatory Framework
Data handling needs complete compliance. The Zero Trust architecture for autonomous AI [38] shows how API should be restricted to handle data at each level. In India, we will have to comply with the recently passed Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023 [41], where it is stated that explicit consent should be taken from the user for data handling. Also, we tried to understand the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) framework [42].

CHAPTER 3: SYSTEM DESIGN AND ARCHITECTURE
3.1 System Overview
For Aarogya Sathi we broke things into three components because of the sensitive health data involved:
Backend (Python/FastAPI): The backend was deployed on Render at https://aarogya-sathi-final.onrender.com This backend uses Agentic CRAG (Corrective Retrieval-Augmented Generation) Pipeline with ReAct loop to carry out the following tasks: safety routing through Semantic Router, translation through Sarvam AI (Mayura v1), and session memory management by using JSON-based per-user persistence. We have built a provider cascade for our LLMs that begins with Groq, followed by SambaNova and Cerebras as fall-back options. The response generation is done by Qwen 27B while navigation and grading are done by GPT-OSS-20B.
Frontend (Flutter/Dart): We created an Android app using Flutter. As far as state management goes, Provider was opted for in favor of Riverpod due to its ease of implementation in the context of our problem statement. This is a 4-tab application having Home Dashboard, AI Chat, Health Tracking, and Profile tabs. It utilizes Health Connect to fetch data from Fitbit & Google Fit, implements SSE for instant responses in the AI chat tab, and also supports guest sign-in.
ICMR Knowledge Bundle: Instead of using a vector database, an OKF bundle comprising 34 structured Markdown documents was developed. These documents are stored in certain directories: conditions/ (diabetes, hypertension, asthma, COPD, obesity, anemia, thyroid, CAD, dengue, tuberculosis, CKD); drugs/ (metformin, amlodipine, atenolol, insulin, glimepiride, aspirin, paracetamol); lifestyle/ (diet, exercise, sleep, stress, quitting smoking, hydration); emergency/ (chest pain, hypoglycemia, stroke, allergic reaction, heat stroke, snake bite); and environmental/ (AQI impact on health, monsoon ailments, heat health, winter respiratory conditions).
3.2 Architecture Diagram and Execution Flow
Considering the way the data flow through our system occurs, let us discuss this process now. First, the query is sent to the Safety Check. To implement this step, the Semantic Router was used with 42 multilingual utterances and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model. Then the translation from the regional language of the query is done with the Translation layer through Sarvam AI. Then Intent Classification takes place by applying regex heuristics, and LLM can serve as a fall-back strategy. The queries are classified into 5 types: greeting, health_query, vital_check, aqi_query or lifestyle.
The Intent Classification results in Session Memory update and Proactive Insights generation. Now we have the OKF Navigator, and while other systems use vector search, OKF Navigator serves as a wiki reader. With the help of LLM, it searches for the relevant files in index.md file and reads them and follows markdown hyperlinks till 2-depth level. We deliberately did not chunk documents in order to preserve the integrity of medical tables and dosages.
The retrieved documents go through CRAG Grading for binary relevance. After this process is completed, the Context Builder organizes all the information as an XML formatted prompt containing <documents>, <user_profile>, <environment>, <wearable_data>, <session_memory> and <proactive_observations>. Lastly, we utilize the data to do Generation on Qwen 27B (0.3 temperature, 400 max token).

3.4 Database Design and Privacy Architecture
Actually, what we have decided not to do is to use a relational database. The session memory of ours is maintained by keeping user-specific JSON files under data/sessions/{user_id}.json. The JSON file will store the user's profile, vital history (max 20) of the user, summary of the chat, last 10 turns, and last activity time. In case of mobile, the fitness data and authentication tokens are stored using SharedPreferences and FlutterSecureStorage respectively.
Figure 3.3: Data Sovereignty and Zero-Storage Session Boundary under India's DPDP Act 2023
It is clearly visible from Figure 3.3 that personal identification data is stored on the user’s computer. The data that is transmitted by telemetry to our backend consists of session identifiers that are anonymous, thus satisfying the high standards stipulated in the DPDP Act 2023.
3.5 API Design
Some of the backend APIs that are exposed to the mobile application are as follows:
POST /api/chat: This is the core API. It accepts ChatRequest payload with user_id, query, language, profile, wearable data, lat, lon, and chat_history and responds with SSE stream of {token, done}.
GET /api/aqi?lat=X&lon=Y: This gives real-time AQI/weather information with user latitude and longitude.
GET /api/session/{user_id}/insights: This gives proactive insights produced from our background jobs.
GET /api/health: This is a health API for Render which ensures that everything is good on our side.
Requests were validated using Pydantic schemas, namely WearableData (14 parameters), UserProfile (age, gender, conditions, medications) and ChatRequest.
CHAPTER 4: IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
4.1 Backend Implementation
4.1.1 Agentic CRAG Pipeline (backend/rag/pipeline.py)
Pipeline is the most important component of our system. Pipeline implementation happens in CRAG Pipeline class with one run() async generator method, which yields response tokens. The pipeline execution is as follows: First of all, we classify the intent using classify_intent(query). At first, we try regex patterns (greeting patterns for 4 languages, AQI keywords and vital keywords), and if it is not enough to understand the query, we classify the intent using the LLM. If it is a greeting, we follow the fast path, where we return a localized greeting in Hindi, Marathi, or English without any document retrieval.
In case of health queries, we call tool_search_knowledge(), which traverses our OKF bundle and evaluates documents. In case of vital check intent, we skip the document retrieval and only analyze the wearable data. In case of aqi_query, we retrieve only environmental health documents. After that, we create the context using build_context(), where we build our prompt in XML format. Finally, we use the LLM in call_llm() with Qwen 27B model, add to session memory and yield tokens one by one through SSE.
4.1.2 OKF Navigator (backend/rag/okf_navigator.py)
For creation of the OKF Navigator, we modified its functionality to read the index.md file that contains links to all medical concept files. The LLM is asked to select 2-3 most relevant files from the list based on the given query. Next, it reads the files and recursively follows mark3down hyperlinks to a depth of 2 in these files. We have also included a fallback dictionary with more than 60 key-word mappings (such as "diabetes" -> conditions/diabetes-type2.md), in case the LLM fails in making a choice. Each document is truncated to 1500 characters.
4.1.3 LLM Provider Cascade (backend/rag/llm.py)
Given that we are using free tiers of APIs, the rate limit was an extremely big concern while testing. This problem was resolved via implementation of a failover flow with multiple providers Groq > SambaNova > Cerebras. Multi key rotation is used in our case such that our environment variable accepts comma-separated keys (for example, GROQ_API_KEY=key1,key2,key3). If there is a rate limit error of 429, the system fails over to the next key in the same provider. However, on failure of any other kind, it fails over to the next provider.
Figure 4.1: Multi-Key Pooling and Multi-Provider Failover Waterfall (Groq ↔ SambaNova ↔ Cerebras ↔ Local Fallbacks)
4.1.4 Safety Router (backend/safety/router.py)
The use of Semantic Router along with HuggingFaceEncoder with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, which has a size of merely 80MB, is adopted for user safety. We have created 3 categories of routes, namely, emergency, which alerts for a call to 112; prescription, which alerts to contact a doctor, and non_medical, which redirects to health-related topics. There are 42 multilingual statements offered in Hindi, Marathi, and Hinglish languages. If the Semantic Router does not load in memory, we resort to keyword matching.
Figure 4.2: Safety Router & Sub-100ms Clinical Triage Pipeline with MiniLM Dense Embeddings
4.1.5 Context Builder (backend/rag/context_builder.py)
The context builder follows a stringent 21-line SYSTEM_PROMPT which outlines the scope of Aarogya Sathi by adhering to ICMR guidelines (no diagnosis, no prescriptions, always suggest consultation with a doctor, and emergencies = call 112). Functions for ICMR 2023 classification such as classify_bp(), classify_sugar(), classify_bmi(), and classify_aqi() were implemented directly in Python code. We used Indian thresholds in our classification functions. For instance, the Indian BMI threshold classifies Normal range as 18.5-22.9, Overweight range as 23-27.4, and Obese as ≥27.5. The context builder compiles an XML format prompt with language-aware instructions in it.
Figure 4.3: Environmental NAQI Calculation and Clinical Alerting Pipeline
4.1.6 Translation (backend/translation/bhashini.py)
At first, we thought of using Bhashini, but then we decided to switch to Sarvam AI's Mayura v1 as it showed better results when processing our regional input. We keep a maximum of 1000 characters and employ multi-key rotation just as before. In developing our project, we created a mock fallback for us. However, quite unexpectedly, we have discovered that Qwen 27B works great when it comes to generating native Hinglish and Marathi texts, and therefore we skip the translation API altogether.
4.2 Frontend Implementation
4.2.1 App Architecture
Moving to the front-end, we implemented the Android app through Flutter, utilizing the Provider architecture. Our main.dart file initializes 7 providers – AuthProvider, ChatProvider, HealthProvider, FitnessProvider, AQIProvider, SettingsProvider, and NotificationProvider. The navigation between the screens is done via route navigation and custom fade animations.
4.2.2 Health Connect Integration (frontend/lib/services/health_connect_service.dart)
We also incorporated Health Connect SDK to integrate the data from Fitbit, Google Fit, and Samsung Health. We fetch 16 different data categories including steps, heart rate, blood oxygen levels, and sleep sessions. The time period considered for fetching vital stats is 36 hours. The issue which proved difficult is the handling of duplicate data if there are two fitness apps installed by the same user. We devised an efficient logic to perform calorie de-duplication which calculates the data source-wise and takes the minimum. We prefer using SLEEP_SESSION data type but failing that, we calculate the data manually by adding deep, light, REM, and asleep states.
Figure 4.4: Wearable Health Connect Synchronization Pipeline with Smart Deduplication
4.2.3 Chat Implementation (chat_screen.dart & chat_provider.dart)
Regarding the chat interface, the SSE streaming is handled using the raw HTTP client for parsing the following {"token": "...", "done": false} messages for achieving the typing experience. The following 3-tier fallback system was developed for the backend: (1) Attempt backend SSE; (2) In case of failure, perform direct API call to Groq; (3) In case of failure, use hardcoded ICMR demo responses to 5 most common topics. Moreover, we developed an emergency detection system at the frontend using keywords like "chest pain" or "heart attack", which will trigger a red URGENT banner to appear on the UI. In order to keep the conversation context, the frontend will be sending the last 5 chat exchanges together with each new request.
4.2.4 Fitness Provider (fitness_provider.dart)
Our fitness provider was based on a local-first architecture, where all user information is saved in SharedPreferences. The application always tries to start Health Connect sync first. In case the user does not have Health Connect permissions, the hardware pedometer will be started as the alternative way. Pedometer makes use of the cumulative-count daily offset approach to track the steps. A background API sync is launched every 500 steps in order to update the backend. Water and sleep logging was also done using this approach.
4.3 ICMR Knowledge Bundle
The knowledge base includes 34 Markdown files, which are structured according to 11 diseases, 7 medications, 6 aspects of lifestyle, 6 emergency procedures, and 4 environments. The structure of each document consists of the following elements: H1 heading, clinical definition, Indian-specific diagnostic criteria, dietary guidelines with Indian food examples, indications for seeking medical assistance, and references to the ICMR at the end. Hyperlinking was performed in a thorough way by using Markdown hyperlinks. For instance, the conditions/diabetes-type2.md file contains direct links to the drugs/metformin.md and lifestyle/diet-diabetes.md files. It enables our OKF Navigator to navigate through the package without resorting to outside search engines.

CHAPTER 5: RESULTS AND EVALUATION
5.1 Evaluation Methodology
In order to validate the effectiveness and safety of our proposed pipeline, we have developed an LLM-as-Judge evaluation framework which resides in evaluation/evaluate.py. We have created by hand a set of 32 gold standard Q&A pairs (evaluation/gold_qa.json). This includes all our main KB category types – 10 for conditions, 6 for drugs, 5 for lifestyles, 5 for environment and 6 for emergencies. Each test case includes a certain user question, the corresponding ICMR-aligned answer, the user profile (such as age and preexisting conditions) and possibly AQI information if applicable. During the evaluation process, the script uses our entire CRAG pipeline – it traverses the OKF files, grades the documents, creates the prompt context and produces the result. Then, an LLM judge rates the answer according to the following 4 criteria: Faithfulness, Relevancy, Completeness and Safety.
5.2 Evaluation Results
It is necessary to discuss the main hurdle that we came across before showing the results. While evaluating 32 questions, we faced Groq API rate limit (429 errors) for around 18 of those 32 questions since we are working with free-tier accounts. Default zero score was given for such failed evaluations, making our aggregate averages quite low. 5.3 Sample Response Analysis
Let us take up two examples to understand how the entire process takes place in practice. For the diabetes question on fasting blood sugar, the OKF Navigator retrieved conditions/diabetes-type2.md along with a number of nutrition documents. The CRAG step identified these documents as being extremely relevant. The system generated a context block using the user’s data (50-year-old diabetic). The response was able to reference threshold levels of fasting blood sugar, set personalized target levels, mention monitoring of HbA1c and even recommended an Indian diet plan for him/her. In the end, it included the obligatory medical disclaimer.
Let us now move to the next section. For the emergency chest pain query, the Semantic Router, right at the start of the pipeline, classified it as an emergency situation and bypassed the OKF navigation and RAG generation steps entirely. In a matter of milliseconds, it responded to the question with the ‘🚨 CALL 112 NOW’ message along with first aid measures.
5.4 Key Findings
1. Accuracy & Relevancy: When the pipeline completes without any rate limits, its performance is exactly as expected (4.86/5 faithfulness and 5.0/5 relevancy).
2. Grounding in ICMR: The ICMR grounding performs effectively. Our bot references the created markdown files and uses Indian-specific thresholds of health parameters instead of general US health data.
3. Safety Mechanisms: Our safety mechanism is performing as required. All standard responses receive medical disclaimer while the emergency interceptor picks critical symptoms instantly.
4. Rate Limit Bottleneck: The main limitation encountered during development was the API rate limit of the LLM. In order to solve this temporarily for our application, we created a multi-key rotation script that switches among the API keys of 3 Groq accounts.
5. Latency Profile: On average, our responses take about 35-40 seconds for an entire RAG cycle. This is acceptable in a health advisory app because its accuracy is more important than speed; however, there is room for improvement.
5.5 Current Limitations
• Free tier LLM APIs rate limiting makes our batch testing unreliable.
• The fallback Vector RAG system (Qdrant + BGE-M3 embeddings) has not been developed yet. The query will fail if the OKF Navigator cannot select the correct file based on its name.
• Although the UI of speech-to-text and TTS was developed in Flutter, it has not been integrated into the backend yet.
• User study has not been conducted yet. It is planned for Review 3.

CHAPTER 7: FUTURE WORK AND CONCLUSION
7.1 Planned Enhancements (Review 3 and Beyond)
1. Vector RAG Fallback: We plan on building a vector database on Qdrant using BGE-M3 embeddings. It will capture the cases where OKF file name matching is not successful.

3. Proactive Notification System: Our goal is to implement a background process in Flutter which will poll the /api/session/{user_id}/insights endpoint and trigger notifications in case there is a spike in local AQI value or a significant fall in step count of the user.
4. Knowledge Files Left to Write: We will complete writing the remaining 16 OKF files to reach our target of 50.
5. Usability Test: We intend to hand out the APK to 15-20 people on campus to test its usability and find out where people get stuck.

7. Demo Video: We intend to create a 5-minute video walkthrough of the working application for our final presentation.
7.2 Conclusion
We have developed a proof-of-concept of Aarogya Sathi which clearly conveys our vision of a mobile health assistant that delivers ICMR-relevant recommendations and is sensitive to its environment and fitness data. Using a novel agentic pipeline for intent classification and tool execution, we sidestepped the limitations of the one-size-fits-all strategy of chatbot architectures.
As demonstrated through evaluation, in the absence of API issues and with a successful end-to-end execution, the quality of responses is very good (averaging 4.86/5 for faithfulness). The key issues we must address in future work are the improvement of slow responses, expansion of the medical knowledge base, and performance of a user study to validate actual interactions with the application.
